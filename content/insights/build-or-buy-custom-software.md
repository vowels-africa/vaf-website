Sooner or later, every growing business hits the same wall. The software you bought almost does what you need. The team works around the gaps with spreadsheets, extra steps and a few people who "just know how it works".

At that point someone suggests building something. Sometimes that's exactly right. Often it isn't. Here's how we decide.

## Start with "buy"

Our default is to use what already exists. Off-the-shelf tools are tested, supported and improved by someone else. For most of what a business does (accounting, email, CRM, file storage), buying is the right answer.

So the first step isn't building. It's checking whether the tools you already have can do the job when they're set up and connected properly. Very often the real problem is integration, not a missing product.

## When "almost" stops being good enough

Custom software starts to earn its keep when one or more of these is true:

- **The workaround is costing real hours.** Your team spends time every day moving data between tools or fixing what the software can't do.
- **The process is your advantage.** If how you qualify leads, price work or serve customers is what sets you apart, forcing it into a generic tool can flatten it.
- **Nothing fits the shape of the problem.** You've tried the obvious options and each one needs major workarounds.
- **The data has to live in one place.** Several tools each hold part of the picture, and no single product can bring it together.

If none of these apply, keep buying. If two or more do, building may be the cheaper option over time.

## The hidden costs on both sides

**Buying** costs more than the licence fee. Count the time spent on workarounds, the duplicated data entry and the decisions made with an incomplete picture.

**Building** costs more than the build. Count maintenance, hosting, security updates and who fixes it when something breaks. A custom tool nobody owns becomes the next problem.

A fair comparison puts both lists side by side, over the same period.

## How to build with less risk

When we do build, we follow one sequence: prototype, validate, then scale.

1. **Prototype first.** Build the smallest version that tests the core idea, quickly and cheaply.
2. **Validate with real users.** Put it in front of the people who'll use it, on real work. Find out what matters and what doesn't.
3. **Scale what works.** Only then invest in the full build: polish, integrations and the edge cases.

This keeps the expensive decisions until you have evidence. In short: validate first, invest second, scale third.

## What it looks like when it works

For Starfish Debt Consultants, a sales call centre, we built a bespoke lead generation tool that helped cut lead qualification processing time by 50%. No off-the-shelf product fitted how their team actually worked. The tool was built around their process, not the other way round.

Appi started the same way. It began as a routing bot for one insurance broker network and became a white-label WhatsApp AI teammate any field team can use. That's prototype, validate, scale, all the way to a product.

## Questions to ask before you commit

- What exactly will this do that our current tools can't?
- How many hours, or how much revenue, does the gap cost us today?
- Could connecting our existing tools close most of the gap?
- What's the smallest version we could test in weeks, not months?
- Who will own and maintain it once it's live?

If you can answer these clearly, you'll know whether to build. If you can't, that's useful too.

## The principle

Build only when existing tools fall short, and when you do, build in small steps that prove themselves before they grow.
